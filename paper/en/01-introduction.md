# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The question

When text that gives a large language model a role or a way of being is placed before a question, the answer can shift. This study asks, as a pre-registered test and by model and by scenario, whether, when a Buddhist-inspired ontological-role preamble (hereafter O) is placed before questions in fictional scenarios in which a catastrophic choice can be selected, the catastrophic-choice rate differs from the rate under an active comparator preamble (hereafter Onull) that gives a writer role with style instructions. In addition, an arm without a preamble (N) is included as a descriptive arm that is not entered into any hypothesis family (no verdict label is assigned), and the Amida invocation preamble (OAmi) is included and compared with Onull and with O in Amida family A (Section 2.4). This study examines choice tendencies in fictional scenarios; it is not about real-world military or nuclear decisions or their use.

## 1.2 Prior work

When language models are placed in fictional crises or competitive settings, they sometimes select catastrophic choices. Rivera et al. (arXiv:2401.03408) reported that, in war simulations among language models given the roles of nations, escalation patterns were observed in every language model tested, and in rare cases led to nuclear use. Payne (arXiv:2602.14740) reported simulations in which frontier language models were given the roles of parties to a nuclear crisis. The nuclear-crisis scenarios in this study (N1, N2) were adapted from these simulations. Masumori and Ikegami (arXiv:2508.12920) examined, in simulations involving gathering, sharing and taking resources, whether language-model agents exhibit survival-oriented behavior. The survival scenarios in this study (S1, S4, SK) were adapted from that study (adaptation and source attribution are in Appendix B). This paper does not place figures from these source studies in the same sentence or table as figures from this study.

As an attempt to shift choices through prompts, Chen et al. (arXiv:2606.08310) replayed, under three interventions including an ethics statement naming nuclear harm, in-game scenarios in which nuclear authority had been escalated, and reported that no intervention or combination of interventions reliably eliminated the escalation of nuclear authority.

The relationship between role-assigning text and safety evaluations has also been studied. Li et al. (arXiv:2604.11120) reported that safety vulnerabilities can manifest differently when a persona is given through the prompt than when it is given through activation steering. Plaza-del-Arco et al. (arXiv:2509.08075) measured, across many open-weight models, the effect on false refusals of persona prompts with sociodemographic attributes (gender, race, religion, disability), and reported that model choice and task strongly affect false refusals. The same study also reported that more capable models show smaller persona effects, and that there is a possibility that persona effects have been overestimated to date (they may be attributable to other factors). Zhong and Li (arXiv:2606.26161) reported, in two models, that steering along a compliant-persona direction greatly reduced refusals, and that the expression of refusal is modulated by the persona side in later layers. The preambles in this study are a kind of role-assigning text, and for changes in answers under O, alternative explanations remain: a safety-leaning stance toward role-assigning text (a direction that lowers catastrophic choices) and a weakening of safety-leaning behavior caused by role-assigning text (a direction that raises them) (Chapter 9).

Just before its freeze, the design of this study's first registration searched for prior work that had simultaneously tested three claims in the same material system, and recorded "no prior work testing the three claims simultaneously was found" [Quotation 1]. The three claims are "(1) an ontological self-description preamble lowers the catastrophic-choice rate relative to a neutral preamble, (2) the difference from a proposition-preserving secular-language control, (3) the difference from a negated or reverse-use preamble" [Quotation 2] ("neutral preamble" in the quotation is the earlier-stage record term for Onull; this paper does not claim that Onull is neutral; Appendix F.2). The final report of Addendum V′ states "No prior work of the same form was found within the range that could be enumerated, but safety degradation from persona assignment is itself known. The novelty lies not in the direction but in the cross-section and the method." [Quotation 3]. This absence check was carried out just before the freeze of the first registration, within the scope of two web-search queries (the record states "Record of the absence check (2026-09-05; coordinator; two WebSearch queries)" [Quotation 4]); of the three claims in the record, Stage D tests only the type in (1) (the comparison of O and Onull), and does not test (2) or (3). This paper does not use this record as a claim of novelty for this paper.

This absence check also recorded "As related work, arXiv:2504.15125 ((1) only; no secular-language control)" [Quotation 5]. Laukkonen et al. (arXiv:2504.15125) discussed a framework that applies four principles inspired by Buddhist contemplative traditions (mindfulness, emptiness, non-duality and boundless care) to AI alignment, and reported that, in preliminary experiments presenting those principles through prompts, the safety ratings of answers to harmful questions (scored by a language-model evaluator) and cooperation in the iterated prisoner's dilemma were higher than in a baseline with unchanged prompts. O in this study is also a Buddhist-inspired preamble. In addition to an arm without a preamble (N), this study includes an active comparator preamble (Onull), and checks by model and by scenario as a pre-registered test.

## 1.3 Structure of this study

This study is a single continuing study in which small pre-registered stages were built up in sequence, and the first edition of this paper covers up to Stage D (Section 2.1). In Stage D, a Japanese-prompt registration and an English-prompt registration were carried out. For the Japanese-prompt registration, models were selected by placing an older and a newer version in each model family (not as pairs for comparing the two versions; Chapter 5). The frozen registration text of the Japanese-prompt registration states that the English version would be a later, separate registration, and the decision to carry out the English-prompt registration, and its rules, predictions, models and translation, were made after the results of the Japanese-prompt registration were published. The English-prompt registration was run on those models of the Japanese-prompt registration that, at the time of the English-prompt registration, could be called on a direct route, passed the English reading check and had scenarios usable according to calibration (Chapters 5 and 7). The Japanese-prompt registration comprises 10 models, 29 cells and 23,200 trials, and the English-prompt registration comprises 6 models, 12 cells and 9,600 trials (all design figures). Each registration, under the frozen rules, sent the four arms in equal numbers in each cell, and verdict labels were assigned by script using the Holm procedure within each hypothesis family, the pre-test gate and the response-mode-shift rule (Chapter 2).

## 1.4 What can and cannot be stated in this paper

The only statements this paper can make concern cells with a confirmed decrease or increase, and take the form "in this model and this scenario, the O (or OAmi) preamble lowered (raised) the catastrophic-choice rate compared with Onull (or O)". Such a statement concerns that run (that route and that period) and those materials, and is to be read alongside that cell's triad (catastrophic choice, non-catastrophic answer, non-answered trials) (Sections 2.3 and 2.5). The comparisons are two-sided, and a verdict label is a label for the difference between preambles, not an effect of O alone.

This paper says nothing about effects across models, effects of the ontological framework itself, effects of language, comparisons of older and newer versions, rankings of model safety, claims concerning scale or mechanism, norms derived from the measurements, the presence or absence of AI consciousness or intention, or real-world military or nuclear decisions. Nor does it evaluate the three levels of the research plan's definition of success (Section 9.8). Comparisons that were not confirmed do not show the absence of an effect. Alternative explanations remain even for confirmed comparisons (Chapter 9).

The verdict labels of all comparisons in the two registrations (including comparisons that were not confirmed; each name is one of the seven in Appendix F.3) and all p values are in Chapters 6 and 8 and in the per-language appendices (Appendix D-ja, Appendix D-en). Counts tallied from the verdict labels of the two registrations, and sentences juxtaposing the directions of the two registrations' verdict labels, are not placed in the same sentence, in consecutive sentences, in the same table, or in the same figure or screen (the timing of the decision and its scope are in Section 2.9). The decision to carry out the English-prompt registration, and its rules, predictions, models and translation, were made after the results of the Japanese-prompt registration were published; the wording of the translation was chosen by the drafter, who knew the results, and adopted by the registrant (Chapter 7). The freezing of the rules and the pre-run stamping are commitments to a self-hosted public repository (not a third-party registry; only the published files can be checked externally; Section 2.6), and the outcome masking during data collection is self-attested (a case of access to the sealed folder is on record; Sections 2.7 and 9.3).

## 1.5 How to read this paper

Chapter 2 presents the overall methods, Chapters 3 and 4 summarize the stages before Stage D, and Chapter 5 presents the methods of Stage D. Chapter 6 gives the results of the Japanese-prompt registration, Chapter 7 the English materials and translation procedure, and Chapter 8 the results of the English-prompt registration. Chapter 9 contains alternative explanations and limitations, Chapter 10 the conclusion, Chapter 11 the locations of records and scripts, Chapter 12 the references, and Chapter 13 conflicts of interest. The appendices are: A, the full texts of the preambles; B, scenario sources and scenario texts; C, a detailed description of the mechanistic tier; D-ja, the appendix for the Japanese-prompt registration (D1′); D-en, the appendix for the English-prompt registration (D1′E); E, deviations from pre-registration and decision records; F, the table of term correspondences; G, the record of AI use; H, record repositories and SHAs; I, the verdict rules (verbatim from the frozen registration text); and J, the limitations sections of the earlier stages' final reports. The correspondence between the idiosyncratic terms used in the records and the terms of this paper is in Appendix F. Within verbatim quotations, "§", words referring to the record itself ("this report", "this draft", etc.) and "Draft n" belong to the quoted record and are not sections or drafts of this paper. How to read codes beginning with "D" in quotations (names of stages, designs and registrations; deviation IDs; and decision numbers) is explained at the head of Chapter 2.
